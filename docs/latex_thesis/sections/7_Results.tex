\section{Results}

All results below come from continuous multi electrode recordings of captive \textit{Electrophorus voltai} in the Museum f{\"u}r Naturkunde Berlin tank. Brazil field recordings were not analysed for this chapter.

\subsection{Special pulse shapes}

Across the processed Berlin dataset ($n = 13{,}465{,}438$ accepted EODs), three shape classes emerge from the RobustPCA plus random forest pipeline (Figure~\ref{fig:pulse_shape_distribution}). Normal pulses dominate (64.0\%, $n = 8{,}623{,}379$). Wide pulses make up about a third (33.5\%, $n = 4{,}514{,}438$). Double pulses are rare (2.4\%, $n = 327{,}621$).

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{pulse_shapes/pulse_shape_distribution.png}
  \caption{How often each RF class shows up in the processed Berlin set. Blue is normal, green wide, red double, with counts and percentages on the bars. Most discharges are normal, about a third are wide, and doubles are uncommon.}
  \label{fig:pulse_shape_distribution}
\end{figure}

Median prototypes capture the morphological differences (Figure~\ref{fig:prototypes}). The normal pulse is a single, roughly symmetric peak with median half width $1.58$\,ms. The wide pulse is a single peak with a slower decay and median half width $4.38$\,ms. The double pulse shows two peaks of similar height separated by a shallow trough. Peak to peak separation on the median double is $2.00$\,ms. Median half width is $4.79$\,ms when the waveform is treated as one event.

Half width distributions back the three way split (Figure~\ref{fig:half_width_overlay}). Normal pulses pile up near $\sim$1.5 to 2\,ms with a second mode around 3 to 3.5\,ms. Wide pulses shift mass toward longer widths (second mode near $\sim$4\,ms). Doubles form a tight peak near 4.5 to 5\,ms. The classes overlap in half width alone, so width is not a sufficient label by itself. The pooled half width histogram without class labels is multimodal at roughly 1.6, 3.2, and 4\,ms (Figure~\ref{fig:half_width_kde}), which is what you would expect if several shape classes sit on top of each other.

Robust PCA of classified waveforms supports the same taxonomy (Figure~\ref{fig:pca_space}). PC1 and PC2 together explain 91.7\% of variance in the plotted sample. Normal pulses sit apart from doubles along PC1/PC2. Wide pulses scatter more and partially overlap the double cloud. Doubles themselves split into two PCA clumps, which hints at more than one double morphology (relative peak heights or inter peak timing) rather than a single stereotype.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{pulse_shapes/labeled_pulses_pca_space.png}
  \caption{RF labelled pulses in Robust PCA space, 5000 examples per class. Blue points are normal, green wide, red double. The four panels are different PC pairings (PC2 vs.\ PC1, PC3 vs.\ PC1, PC3 vs.\ PC2, PC4 vs.\ PC1). Normals sit in a fairly tight cloud away from the doubles. Wides sprawl and lean into the double region. The doubles themselves look like two clumps rather than one.}
  \label{fig:pca_space}
\end{figure}

Aligning two median normal pulses to the two peaks of the median double shows that the double envelope is close to two normals fired about 2\,ms apart (Figure~\ref{fig:normal_aligned_double}). A formal two normal template fit (model A) to the median double recovers a similar separation ($\Delta t = 2.22$\,ms) with $R^2 = 0.853$ (Figure~\ref{fig:double_template_fit}). The fit is sharper in the trough than the empirical median, so the double is \emph{approximately} two overlapping normals, not a perfect sum. Comparing model A against wider free width families on the same median prototype does not change that qualitative picture (Figure~\ref{fig:double_model_hierarchy}).

\end{figure}

\subsection{Activity across timescales}

Effort normalized pulse rates for all EODs, double pulses, and wide pulses are shown together at four timescales in Figure~\ref{fig:activity_all_shapes}. Medians are active session medians with bootstrap 95\% CI shading.

On the 24\,h scale (Figure~\ref{fig:activity_all_shapes}, top left), Berlin \textit{E.\ voltai} are day active. All pulse rate sits near $\sim$0.6\,Hz through the night (00:00 to 04:00), rises after about 05:00, and peaks near 08:00 to 09:00 at roughly 1.55\,Hz. After that morning peak the median falls to about 1.1\,Hz by 10:00 and then wanders between roughly 0.75 and 1.0\,Hz for the rest of the day. Double and wide medians stay near zero across the clock. Wide pulses show only a faint bump under the morning all pulse peak. That morning maximum lines up with feeding related activity (Section~\ref{sec:feeding_corr}) more than with a flat day plateau.

On the calendar month scale (Figure~\ref{fig:activity_all_shapes}, top right), all pulse medians sit around 0.6 to 1.0\,Hz for most of the year, with a wide March confidence band and a clear late year peak in November ($\sim$1.7\,Hz) before a drop in December. Wide pulses track that November bump: they are near zero from January through September, rise in October, and reach about 0.6\,Hz in November. Double pulses stay flat near zero all year. Whether the November all pulse / wide pulse peak tracks a rainy season pattern is unclear from tank data alone \notels{rainy season interpretation: keep, drop, or rephrase}.

Months since recording start (Figure~\ref{fig:activity_all_shapes}, bottom left) show that early 2024 carried the highest all pulse medians, with peaks near $\sim$6\,Hz in May 2024 and $\sim$5.5\,Hz in July 2024. Rates then sit lower, with smaller peaks around January 2025 ($\sim$1.6\,Hz) and November 2025 ($\sim$2.4\,Hz). Gaps appear where no sessions contribute (including a break around early to mid 2025). Wide pulses are almost absent until the November 2025 spike ($\sim$1.4\,Hz), which is the chronological source of the November bump in the calendar month panel. Double pulses stay low, with a small rise late in the record (on the order of 0.5\,Hz into early 2026). Special shapes therefore do not simply mirror overall EOD rate. They arrive as sparse bursts on top of the day active baseline.

Yearly bins (Figure~\ref{fig:activity_all_shapes}, bottom right) show a steady drop in all pulse median rate from about 3.5\,Hz in 2023 through about 1.7\,Hz (2024) and 1.0\,Hz (2025) to about 0.5\,Hz in 2026. Double and wide yearly medians remain near $\sim$0.1\,Hz and do not follow that long term decline. Partial year coverage and dual line weighting (Methods) still matter when reading the yearly drop.

  \caption{Pulse rate across four timescales, with all three classes in the same panels. Thick traces are active session medians (light blue all pulses, pink doubles, dark green wides), the washed out bands around them are bootstrap 95\% CIs, and the faint dots are the underlying session bins. Clockwise from top left: a clear daytime rise with a morning all pulse peak, a November bump that wide pulses help drive, the chronological record with early 2024 highs and a November 2025 wide spike, and a year by year drop in overall rate while the special shapes stay near the floor.}

\subsection{Environmental and feeding correlations}
\label{sec:feeding_corr}

Same day scatter of pulse rate against tank temperature and conductivity shows little structure in the measured ranges (Figure~\ref{fig:env_corr}). Temperature spans only about 25.4 to 26.8\,$^{\circ}$C. Conductivity spans about 180 to 350\,$\mu$S\,cm$^{-1}$. Regression lines for all pulses are nearly flat. Wide and double rates sit much lower and likewise lack a convincing linear link. With small $n$ of environmental samples, this is a weak test, not proof of independence.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{correlations/correlations_all_pulse_shapes.png}
  \caption{Does pulse rate track tank temperature or conductivity on the same day? Each cloud is a pulse class (light blue all, pink double, dark green wide), with a dashed line of the same colour for the linear fit. Temperature is on the left, conductivity on the right. Nothing much tilts in either panel within these narrow ranges, and the special shape rates sit well below the all pulse scatter.}
  \label{fig:env_corr}
\end{figure}

Lagged Spearman correlations over 0 to 30 days are noisier (Figure~\ref{fig:lag_corr}). Wide pulses show large swings versus conductivity (positive near a 3 day lag, negative near 14 days). Double pulses flip sign within a few days for both variables. All pulse lags stay closer to zero. These delayed peaks should be read cautiously until sample size and multiple comparison structure are tightened \notels{decide which lagged peaks are worth discussing in Discussion}.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{correlations/lag_corr_all_pulse_shapes.png}
  \caption{Spearman $\rho$ between pulse rate and environment as a function of lag (0 to 30 days). Light blue, pink, and dark green mark all, double, and wide pulses (circles on the traces). The dashed horizontal line is zero correlation. Conductivity is above, temperature below. Wide pulses swing hard with conductivity lag, doubles flip sign within a few days, and the all pulse curve stays closer to flat.}
  \label{fig:lag_corr}
\end{figure}

Feeding leaves a clearer footprint. Mean pulse rate in a $\pm$5\,min window around feeding is about 5\,Hz for all pulses, versus about 1.3\,Hz more than 5\,min away from feeding (Figure~\ref{fig:feeding_bars}). Wide pulses rise modestly. Double pulses barely move on this scale. Peri feeding trajectories show the rise starting about 2\,min before the logged feeding time, peaking near 10\,Hz at time zero, then decaying over the next 10\,min (Figure~\ref{fig:feeding_traj}). Mean feeding clock time is 17:17 \notels{replace 17:17 with real mean feeding time $\pm$ CI from the notes pipeline}. That morning activity peak in Figure~\ref{fig:activity_all_shapes} lines up with feeding more than with a pure circadian drive.

Within each hue the darker bar is feeding and the lighter one is baseline. Overall activity jumps during feeding, wides go up a bit, and doubles barely leave the axis.}

\end{figure}

\subsection{Position estimation}

Head position was taken as the electrode with the largest positive peak in each multi channel snippet. Collapsed over all hourly bins, occupancy is strongly end biased (Figure~\ref{fig:pos_overall}). Pulse rate peaks at electrode 0 (bright end, $\sim$0.39\,Hz) and electrode 15 (dark end, $\sim$0.43\,Hz). Mid bright electrodes (1 to 9) stay low. Dark side electrodes 10 to 14 carry more mid line activity than the bright mid line. The animals park at tank ends and use the dark half more while moving between ends.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{position_estimation/overall_position_distribution_peak.png}
  \caption{Where along the 16 electrode line the pulses land when hourly bins are collapsed. Blue bars give pulse rate per electrode. The dashed vertical line is the bright/dark cut at 2.5\,m, and the light grey wash marks the dark half (electrodes 10 to 15). Animals spend most time at the two ends (0 and 15), and the dark mid electrodes stay busier than the bright mid stretch.}
  \label{fig:pos_overall}
\end{figure}

Session wise median head position across timescales is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pos_panels}. On the 24\,h scale the median stays mostly on the bright side of the 2.5\,m boundary, dipping toward the front (lower meters) in the morning around 07:00 to 08:00 and sitting closer to the dark boundary at other hours. That matches the working picture that daytime use skews toward the bright front while nights and quieter periods pull animals toward the dark back, though the median alone understates how often the upper percentile band crosses into the dark half. Calendar month and months since start show slow drifts of preferred position, including a late 2025 push toward the dark boundary.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{position_estimation/position_panels_peak.png}

The pink line is the session wise median, the light pink band the 16th to 84th percentile, and the faint blue dots the session values themselves. Everything above the dashed line (grey wash) is the dark half of the tank past 2.5\,m. Medians usually sit on the bright side, slide toward the front mid morning, and wander over months, including a late 2025 stretch nearer the dark boundary.}

\end{figure}
