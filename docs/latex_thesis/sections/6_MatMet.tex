\section{Materials and Methods}

Field hardware was built and deployed for a four week expedition to Brazil in September 2025 (two weeks in Manaus at Reserva Ducke and INPA, two weeks near Macap{\'a} on the Rio Araguari and Rio Falsino in FLONA). 
The quantitative results in this thesis use continuous multi electrode recordings from captive \textit{Electrophorus} at the Museum f{\"u}r Naturkunde Berlin (Natural History Museum Berlin).

\subsection{Recording hardware}

Recordings used custom multi electrode loggers based on Teensy microcontrollers with 16 channel amplifier boards. 
Software and board designs are available at \url{https://github.com/janscience/TeeGrid}, \url{https://github.com/janscience/TeeRec}, and \url{https://github.com/janscience/Teensy_Amp}. 
Each device digitizes 16 electrode channels continuously to disk as multi channel WAV files.

Electrodes were wound by hand from stainless steel wire (three turns). 
Three array geometries were built: lines, square grids, and one tetrahedron for exploratory experiments. 
The tetrahedron will not be discussed further here. 
Grid frames were assembled from PVC tubing with glued on connectors.
This way the construction was foldable for transport while still being rubust and quick to deploy. 
Details of frame carpentry are omitted here beyond the geometries below.

\textbf{Grids.} Square frames with 180\,cm side length carried 16 electrodes in a $4 \times 4$ layout at 60\,cm spacing. 
PVC tubes form the edges. 
Straight segments use sleeved  plug-in PVC joints. 
Corners use PVC cross pieces. 
The packed frame folds to 60\,cm edge length.

\textbf{Lines.} Flexible electrode lines use a cable bundle wrapped in spiral tubing. 
Total length is 15.6\,m. 
The first electrode sits 150\,cm from the housing end. 
The remaining electrodes follow at 80\,cm spacing, totalling 16 electrodes. 
Flexibility reduces mechanical damage in the field and simplifies packing relative to rigid frames.

\subsubsection{Assembly}

For grids, tubes were laid out in the target configuration, electrodes slipped onto the tubes, and cable runs cut with slack for joints plus about 150\,cm from grid to housing. 
Stretchy ropes (four on inner bars, two on cross bars) tension the frame. 
Cables were soldered to the matching electrodes, sealed with waterproof heat shrink, and routed under spiral tubing along cable bearing tubes.

For lines, 16 cables were cut to length, bundled, and wrapped in spiral tubing with electrode positions marked. 
At each site the matching cable was pulled out of the wrap, the electrode wound onto tubing and cable, then soldered. 
Waterproof heat shrink sealed each joint. 
Spiral wrap was applied in inter electrode segments with about 20\,cm overlap at electrodes for stability. 
Tube diameter decreased toward the free end as fewer cables remained.

The cable to housing transition used a threaded gland.
To ensure this is was water proof, heat shrink was applied over the 16 cable bundle
Then the gland volume was filled with epoxy resin, avoiding air bubbles and epoxy leakage that would stiffen the free cables or interfere with their fit in the housing.


\subsubsection{Waterproofing and power}

Solder joints were sealed with waterproof heat shrink. 
Electronics sat in PVC housings. 
These housings were waterproofed with threaded end caps and O rings. 
Before closing, o rings were cleaned andsilicone paste was used to insure seamless sealing. 
The outside to inside cable interface was potted in epoxy as above.

Battery packs were built from seven Lithium ion cells wired in parallel via nickel sheet on like poles (positive to positive, negative to negative)
A protection circuit was soldered on after corrosion protection spray. 
Contacts were kept from overheating during soldering by using minimal temperature and heat exposure time. 
Each pack was heat shrunk, capped with cut PVC plates, heat shrunk again, and labeled. 
Packs were stored at about 3.4\,V rest charge. 
Capacity is 24.5\,Ah per pack, within typical hand luggage flight limits, for about 60\,h or 2.5 days of logger runtime.

\subsection{Berlin captive recordings}

Berlin data were recorded in a dual pool tank, two pools of about 3\,m diameter joined by an opening of about 2\,m. 
In the Berlin setting most recordings were obtained using a 16 electrode line with 25\,cm spacing, totalling 3.75\,m. 
Channel 0 which was closest to the logger was located in the bright area.
The bright versus dark boundary used for occupancy analyses sits at about 2.5\,m. 
A second line was added in November 2025 to span bright and dark regions of the tank.
Dual line recordings from 25 November 2025 onward enter rate analyses with pulse counts weighted by 0.5.
Yearly panels report session-median rates (Hz), so partial calendar years 2023 and 2026 are not scaled by the fraction of the year recorded.
Those years remain samples of the months that were actually logged.
The Berlin Dataset spans 13.7 TiB of raw WAV recordings obtained from 2023 to 2026, with around 500 GiB of processed data in NIX/H5 format. 

\subsection{Video and audio synchronization}
\label{sec:video_sync}

Where video was available, logger time and camera time were aligned with this open source tool at \url{https://github.com/weygoldt/videosync}. 
An LED on the logger blinks irregularly with a known schedule. 
videosync uses a video region of interest (ROI) to track the blinking LED on the logger.
Blink timing is recovered from logger metadata. 
Then both traces are cross correlated to estimate the offset. Highest cross correlation value is used to align the two time bases.
Logger audio was band passed with 100 to 3000\,Hz, then stitched across overlapping WAV segments and muxed onto the video for later behavioral scoring.

\subsection{Data analysis}
\notels{elaborate more in this whole section?}

Berlin WAV recordings were processed from peak detection through the analyses used in the Results. 
Video sync (Section~\ref{sec:video_sync}) is optional for anecdotal behavioural scoring and is not required for the pulse rate pipeline.

\subsubsection{Peak detection and EOD labeling}

Continuous WAVs were processed with eeltracker (\url{https://github.com/weygoldt/eeltracker}). 
Peaks are detected at low threshold on each channel (\texttt{scipy.signal.find\_peaks}, typical prominence and refractory settings on the order of a few digital units and a few milliseconds), 
grouped into multi channel pulse events with short waveform cutouts, and written to NIX/H5. 
A diverse seed set is labeled by hand as EOD versus noise. 
An InceptionTime ensemble classifier is trained on those labels and applied to the full recording, storing \texttt{predicted\_labels} (0 = noise, 1 = EOD) with pulse centers and raw snippets. 
Downstream analyses keep only events with \texttt{predicted\_labels} $= 1$.

\subsubsection{Special pulse classification}

On the predetected H5 pulses, a supervised classifier marks two special shapes used throughout the Results: double pulses and wide pulses. 
Waveforms are projected with RobustPCA (at most 20 components) and classified with a random forest trained on manually labeled examples. 
Rare classes (wide, double) are accepted only if their predicted probability is at least 0.40. 
Markers \texttt{is\_double\_peak}, \texttt{is\_wide\_pulse}, and \texttt{special\_pulse\_class} are written back beside each H5 file. 
Rule based width and double peak metrics remain available for diagnostics but are not the production decision.

\subsubsection{Activity rates}

Pulse times are binned at minute, hour, day, month, month since recording start, and year. 
Minute and day bins were discarded in the final analysis because they did not add information beyond the hour and month bins.
Rates are counts divided by recording effort in each bin, separately for all EODs, double pulses, and wide pulses.
The multi-timescale panels in Results are active-session medians of those per-session rates, with bootstrap 95\% CIs, not a single pooled rate per bin.

\subsubsection{Pulse shape characterization}

Half maximum widths are collected by shape class. 
Mean and median prototype waveforms are formed for normal, double, and wide pulses. 
Shape prevalence is summarized over the dataset. 
The median double pulse prototype is fit as the sum of two median normal pulse templates. 
Six nested models (A--F) were compared (Figure~\ref{fig:double_model_hierarchy}), relaxing constraints on the inter-component delay $\Delta t$ and on the component widths in each model. 
%Models A--D keep native or near-native widths, with $\Delta t$ either free or locked to the observed 1\,ms peak spacing; 
%E and F additionally let the two components widen (shared width in E, independent widths in F). 
Model E was chosen as the working description as it recovers the two-peak envelope and trough of the median double, with free $t_1$ and $t_2$ and shared but free width (w).
Amplitudes $a_1$ and $a_2$ are also free.
The extra independent-width freedom in F didn't add relevant improvement. 
Labeled pulses are also shown in PCA space, and half width, inter pulse interval, and double pulse rate are tracked over time.

Temperature and conductivity from tank water logs are correlated with daily and monthly rates.

Feeding windows ($\pm$5\,min around logged feeding times) are contrasted with baseline minutes ($>$5\,min from feeding) using mean pulse rates and a two-sided Mann--Whitney $U$ test on the minute-level rates; peri-feeding trajectories are also computed.
Mean feeding clock time is the circular mean of the first logged feeding-related timestamp per session, with a bootstrap 95\% CI.

\subsubsection{Position along the electrode line}

For each accepted pulse, head position along the Berlin line is taken as the electrode with the largest positive peak amplitude in the multi channel snippet (25\,cm bins). 
Occupancy histograms and mean position are computed on the same timescales as the activity rates, including bright versus dark occupancy relative to the 2.5\,m boundary. 
Collapsed occupancy rates are electrode pulse counts divided by total recording seconds (the sum of day of year recording time bins).
