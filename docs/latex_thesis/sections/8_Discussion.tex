\section{Discussion}

The practical outcome of this thesis is a multi-year record of captive \textit{Electrophorus voltai} electric organ discharges. The recordings cover pulse shapes, pulse rates across several timescales, coarse space use along an electrode line, and the response of pulse rate to feeding. They also include the hardware development and field work that made these recordings possible. In that sense, the thesis is as much a methodological contribution as an ethological one.

The title of the thesis still points towards social and courtship behaviour in the natural habitat, but that question is not answered by the Berlin dataset. Wild social sequences, nest related signalling, and group hunting remain open questions. The present analysis is based on the captive colony at the Museum für Naturkunde Berlin. A longer term aim is to compare these recordings with \textit{E.\ voltai} recorded around Macapá and in FLONA, including the Rio Araguari and Rio Falsino and the Ducke recordings. Applying the same processing pipeline to the field recordings should make it possible to separate patterns that are specific to captivity from patterns that are more consistent across contexts. The two datasets are therefore best treated as complementary rather than as substitutes for one another.

The most distinct morphological result is the separation of the recorded EODs into three shape classes. Across more than thirteen million accepted EODs, the classifier identifies normal, wide, and double pulses. These labels describe waveform shape. They do not by themselves identify behavioural acts.

The half width distributions already show why shape and behaviour should not be equated. Normal and wide pulses overlap substantially around 1.5 to 2 ms, while doubles form a narrower ridge around 2.4 ms. Width therefore contains useful information, but it does not provide a clean one dimensional separation between the classes. The Robust PCA gives a similar picture in a different representation of the waveform data. In particular, double pulses form a relatively compact group, but the higher principal component views suggest two clumps along PC1. This may reflect more than one double morphology, for example differences in relative peak height or inter-peak timing, rather than a single stereotyped waveform.

The median double pulse can also be approximated by two normal pulses separated by about one millisecond. When two median normal pulses are aligned with the two peaks of the median double, their overlap produces a trough close to the one observed in the double pulse. A two-normal template fit gives a similar separation of $\Delta t = 1.18$ ms compared with the observed peak spacing of 1.00 ms, with $R^2 = 0.978$. Allowing the component widths to vary improves the fit further. The important point is not that the double is generated by two normal pulses, which the present data cannot establish, but that its waveform is consistent with a short doublet of otherwise normal pulses. The fitted waveform still differs slightly from the empirical median, in particular around the trough, so this should be treated as an approximation rather than a mechanistic explanation.

This raises the more difficult question of what the double pulses represent. They could reflect a genuine feature of the electromotor system, but overlap between nearby discharges or classification effects remain plausible alternatives. High voltage doublets have been described during prey capture, where they can trigger prey movement before the suction strike \parencite{catania2014shocking}. Later work expanded this sequence into volleys with precise spatial coordination \parencite{catania2015highvoltage, catania2015electric, catania2019astonishing}. \textcite{xu2021third} also described a third discharge form and showed more generally that the main, Hunter's, and Sachs' organs should not be treated as functionally interchangeable.

One possible physiological route to a double waveform would therefore involve different contributions from the electric organs. A single command might produce one component, while a short bout recruits an additional organ and generates a second component on the electrode about a millisecond later. This is speculative. Work on weakly electric fish shows that ion channel kinetics can affect electrocyte spike duration and therefore EOD pulse width \parencite{mcanelly2000coregulation}, which makes a physiological contribution to waveform fine structure plausible, but it does not provide a direct explanation for the double pulses observed here. The membrane physiology described for electric eels much earlier remains relevant background for the same reason \parencite{keynes1953membrane}.

For the Berlin dataset, I therefore interpret the three classes cautiously. Normal pulses are consistent with the common low voltage discharge. Wide pulses appear more closely related to the same single peaked waveform and could reflect state, signal quality, or individual variation rather than a distinct behavioural act. Double pulses remain more difficult to interpret. Their waveform resembles the short doublets described in the context of hunting, but the similarity alone is not sufficient to assign the Berlin double class to feeding behaviour. The feeding analysis provides some evidence against such a simple interpretation because double pulse rates do not increase strongly around feeding events. Distinguishing genuine doublets from overlapping normal pulses will require recordings linked to individual identity, pulse amplitude, and behaviour, ideally with video tagged events.

The feeding response is much clearer. Mean all-pulse rate in the $\pm$5 min window around logged feeds is about four times the rate outside that window. The rate begins to rise roughly two minutes before the logged feeding time and reaches close to 10 Hz around time zero before declining over the following ten minutes. This pattern also helps explain the morning maximum in the 24 h activity plot. The timing is consistent with the animals increasing their pulse rate before food arrives, which could reflect anticipation of the feeding schedule or responses to cues from the keeper.

The feeding response should not be interpreted as evidence for hunting volleys in the Berlin tank. Double pulse rates change little in the same analysis. The strongest conclusion is more limited. Feeding produces a marked increase in overall pulse rate, while the special pulse classes do not show the same response. A useful follow-up would be to alter or omit feeding times and test whether the morning pulse rate peak shifts with the schedule.

Environmental variables provide a less clear result. Same-day correlations between pulse rate, temperature, and conductivity are weak. This is not surprising for temperature because the measured range in the tank is narrow. A weak correlation within that range cannot be taken as evidence that pulse rate is independent of temperature more generally. Half width may be a more informative variable because spike duration is temperature sensitive in electric fish, and the Berlin width versus temperature analysis is not yet complete.

The calendar month plot shows a clear November peak in all-pulse and wide-pulse rates, but the chronological analysis shows that much of this increase comes from a wide-pulse spike in November 2025. Without additional husbandry information it would be premature to interpret this as evidence for a seasonal or rainy-season pattern. The tank is not the floodplain, and the calendar position of a peak does not by itself establish an environmental driver.

The long-term decline in median all-pulse rate is also difficult to interpret.
Median rate decreases from about 3.5\,Hz in 2023 to about 0.5\,Hz in 2026.
The drop is large enough that it needs an explanation, and several non-exclusive ones remain.
Dual-line pulse counts are already weighted by 0.5 from 25 November 2025, so the plotted decline is not the raw effect of adding a second line.
2023 and 2026 still cover only part of the calendar year, and the number of animals in the tank is not held constant.
Aging, habituation, and slow hardware drift are also plausible.
Special shapes do not simply follow that decline, which already hints that the drop is not a uniform quieting of every discharge class.

The next step here is to compare the yearly rate with variables that can help distinguish these explanations. Recording effort and the number of animals contributing to each period are needed first. Median amplitude would also be useful. A decline in both amplitude and rate would make recording geometry or hardware effects more plausible, whereas a stable amplitude alongside a declining rate would leave behavioural or demographic explanations more likely. The current data are enough to identify the long-term change, but not to assign its cause.

Space use in the Berlin dataset is necessarily coarse. Head position is estimated from the electrode with the largest positive peak. This works best when one animal dominates a short recording segment and is oriented reasonably along the electrode line. It becomes less reliable when several animals discharge at once or when an animal lies parallel to the array.

Even with those limitations, the collapsed occupancy shows a preference for the tank ends. The darker middle electrodes are used more often than the brighter middle section. Session-wise medians also shift across the day and across months, including a movement towards the dark boundary late in 2025. These patterns are worth describing, but they should not yet be interpreted as evidence for a day-night habitat cycle. One possible husbandry explanation is that the bright end is also the keeper and feeding side. The morning movement towards that end therefore occurs alongside the feeding response, but the present data do not show that this reflects a circadian habitat preference.

The spatial scale of the tank also limits comparison with the natural environment. Field observations describe adult electric eels using much larger and more structured habitats than the museum tank \parencite{assunccao1995reproduction, bastos2020historia}. The current analysis can therefore identify preferred regions within the array, but it does not provide a wild home range. Improvements to the localisation procedure, including further development of the eeltracker processing chain, would make it possible to reconstruct trajectories rather than only occupancy frequencies. That would provide a much stronger basis for comparison with field observations.

This is one reason to keep the Berlin and FLONA or Ducke recordings within the same analytical framework. The captive dataset is particularly valuable for long continuous recording, for establishing the waveform classes, for obtaining enough rare double pulses to examine their morphology, and for controlled comparisons around feeding. It cannot resolve behaviours that are unlikely to occur in the tank, including migration, nest building, or the group hunting behaviour reported in the field \parencite{bastos2021social}. The reproductive biology of armoured catfish may offer useful comparative ideas, but it remains an analogy rather than evidence for a corresponding mechanism in \textit{Electrophorus} \parencite{hostache1998reproductive}. Applying the same classification and activity analysis to the FLONA and Ducke \textit{E.\ voltai} recordings is therefore the most direct next step.

The recording system itself is an important part of what makes these analyses possible. Pulse amplitudes measured on the grid fall within the 15 to 50 V range reported by \textcite{xu2021third}, which provides a useful check that passive non-invasive electrodes recover the expected voltage scale. The TeeGrid, TeeRec, and Teensy amplifier system, together with the waterproofing and battery solutions developed for the expedition work, provide a basis for extending the same approach beyond the captive setting.

There are also broader practical implications, but these should be kept separate from what the present data demonstrate. Captive breeding could reduce the need for wild-caught animals, but the breeding claim itself still requires verification. More detailed behavioural observations are needed before drawing conservation conclusions. The Berlin colony is useful as a controlled and long-term recording system, but it is not a substitute for studying Amazonian populations.

\subsection{Conclusions and Outlook}

The Berlin recordings provide a long-term captive EOD dataset with a reproducible separation into three waveform classes. The feeding analysis shows a clear increase in overall pulse rate around scheduled feeding, including a rise before the logged feeding time. Coarse localisation reveals non-uniform use of the tank, although the present method is not precise enough to reconstruct natural space use. Together with the recording hardware and processing pipeline, these results establish a useful baseline for the field recordings.

Several questions remain unresolved. The waveform classes should not yet be interpreted as behavioural categories, and the double pulses in particular require recordings linked to individual animals, amplitude, and behaviour before they can be related to hunting or other specific actions. The cause of the long-term decline in overall pulse rate is also unclear, as are the possible effects of temperature once the narrow range of the Berlin data is considered more carefully.

The immediate analytical priorities are therefore to complete the half-width analysis against temperature, compare the long-term rate decline with recording effort and amplitude, and examine the double pulses with behavioural and identity information. The same classification and activity pipeline can then be applied to the FLONA and Ducke recordings. The main value of the Berlin dataset is not that it resolves the natural behaviour of \textit{E.\ voltai}, but that it provides a detailed reference against which recordings from the natural environment can be interpreted.
